Stats and Decay

If nobody plays with your pet, what happens to it?

If nobody plays with your pet, what happens to it?

Think about a real pet — a dog left alone all day, a plant that hasn't been watered, a fish tank nobody checks. Things get worse on their own. The world drains things. That's not a bug — it's the whole point.

Today you're going to build the engine that makes a virtual pet feel alive: a decay loop that runs whether you're watching or not, and stat bars that show you exactly how things are going.

Variables as stat containers

Your creature has three stats — food, happy, and energy. Each one is a number from 0 to 100. Right now, before anything happens, you declare them:

let food = 80; // 80 out of 100 — not hungry yet let happy = 70; // 70 out of 100 — pretty happy let energy = 60; // 60 out of 100 — some energy left

Those three lines are a complete snapshot of how your creature is doing right now. They are the source of truth. Every bar on screen, every change in expression, every bit of behavior — it all comes from these three numbers.

When you feed the creature, food goes up. When it runs around, energy goes down. When you pet it, happy goes up. Simple rules, interesting results.

setInterval — "do this every N milliseconds"

Now here is the key question: how does food go down on its own, even if the player does nothing?

The answer is setInterval. It is a built-in JavaScript function that runs a block of code over and over again, on a timer. You tell it two things: what to do, and how long to wait between runs.

setInterval(function() { // this block runs every 5000 milliseconds (5 seconds) food = Math.max(0, food - 2); happy = Math.max(0, happy - 1); energy = Math.max(0, energy - 1.5); }, 5000); // 5000 ms = 5 seconds

Every five seconds, those three lines run. Food drops by 2. Happy drops by 1. Energy drops by 1.5. The creature is always getting a little worse, all by itself — unless you do something about it.

Math.max(0, ...) — the safety floor

Look at this part closely:

food = Math.max(0, food - 2);

Why not just write food = food - 2? The problem is that subtraction keeps going. If food is 1 and you subtract 2, you get -1. Then -3. Then -5. Your bar would try to be -5% wide, which breaks everything.

Math.max(0, food - 2) means: "take whichever is bigger — zero, or the result of food minus 2." So if food - 2 would be negative, you get 0 instead. The stat hits the floor and stays there.

Why Math.max? Math.max(0, food - 2) makes sure the number never goes below zero. Without it, stats would go negative and your bars would break — or worse, they'd wrap around and show 100% when the creature is actually starving.

What happens at zero

A number alone doesn't do anything. You have to write the code that checks the number and does something based on it. This is the draw loop — the part of the program that looks at the current stats and decides how to draw the creature.

// inside the draw loop — runs ~60 times per second if (food < 20) { // creature looks pale and droopy drawBlob(paleColor, droopyEyes); } else if (happy > 75) { // creature bounces and glows drawBlob(brightColor, bouncyOffset); } else { // creature looks fine drawBlob(normalColor, neutralEyes); }

Two separate loops run at the same time: the setInterval decay loop that changes the numbers every few seconds, and the requestAnimationFrame draw loop that reads those numbers and draws the creature up to 60 times per second. They never directly talk to each other — the shared variables are the messenger.

The heart of every virtual pet: the decay loop is the heart of every virtual pet. The world drains things. You are the one who refills. That tension — drain vs. restore — is what makes a tamagotchi feel alive. Without decay, there is no reason to care.
